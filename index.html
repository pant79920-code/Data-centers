Why does this matter?

Data centers are nearly invisible to the public — vast, windowless buildings humming quietly on the edges of towns. But a single large hyperscale campus can consume more electricity than a mid-size city and more water per day than many municipal water systems. These facilities receive billions in public tax subsidies with no environmental strings attached, and are disproportionately sited near communities that already carry the highest pollution burdens.

Plain-English Guide

What is a data center — and why should Illinois residents care?

A guide to data centers, their resource footprint, the public money behind them, and what's changing

What is a data center?

A data center is a large, purpose-built facility filled with rows of computer servers — the physical machines that store, process, and transmit information powering the modern internet. Every time you stream video, send a message, shop online, or use an AI assistant, your data travels through a data center somewhere. These are not abstract "clouds" — they are real buildings consuming real electricity and real water, located in real Illinois communities.

The largest ones, called hyperscale data centers, are operated by a handful of technology giants: Meta, Google, Amazon Web Services, and Microsoft. A single hyperscale campus can span several million square feet — the footprint of 40 or 50 Walmart Supercenters — and house hundreds of thousands of servers running around the clock, 365 days a year. These machines never sleep and never reduce their cooling needs.

In Illinois specifically: There are more than 200 operational data centers, making Illinois one of the top five data center markets in the United States. The heaviest concentration is in Chicago's suburban ring — particularly Aurora and Montgomery in Kane County, DeKalb in DeKalb County, and Elk Grove Village in Cook County — where abundant land, favorable zoning, major fiber optic corridors, and one of the most generous state tax incentive programs in the country have made Illinois exceptionally attractive for hyperscale investment over the past decade.

Why do they use so much water?

Servers generate enormous amounts of heat as a byproduct of computation — and heat is the single greatest enemy of reliable computing. To prevent systems from crashing, virtually all large data centers use industrial cooling systems running continuously, 24 hours a day, every day of the year.

The most common method at hyperscale facilities is evaporative cooling: water is circulated through massive cooling towers and allowed to evaporate, carrying heat away from the building. The physics are exactly the same as sweating — just scaled to industrial magnitude. The water that evaporates does not go back. It is permanently removed from the local water system, dispersed into the atmosphere, gone.

To understand the scale: A single 100 MW data center using evaporative cooling consumes roughly 5 million gallons of water per day under typical Illinois conditions — approximately equal to the entire daily water use of a city of 50,000 people. Meta's DeKalb Campus I operates at 800 MW. Combined with Meta's other Illinois facilities, Meta alone is estimated to consume enough water daily to supply a city larger than Springfield. Unlike Springfield's municipal water system — required to report consumption in granular public detail — Meta faces no legal obligation to disclose a single gallon.
The disclosure gap: Unlike most industrial water users in Illinois, data centers face no mandatory public disclosure requirements. Factories, power plants, and agricultural irrigators above certain thresholds must report their water use to state agencies. Data centers do not. Every water figure in this tracker is a modeled estimate based on a peer-reviewed academic formula — representing the best publicly available data in the absence of required disclosure.

What are the tax incentives — and who benefits?

In 2019, the Illinois General Assembly created the Data Center Investment Program, administered by DCEO. Companies that certify qualifying investments receive exemptions from Illinois state sales tax on data center equipment and the electricity to power it. For a large hyperscale campus, these exemptions can be worth hundreds of millions of dollars over the life of the facility.

The case for the incentives: Hyperscale campuses represent genuine large-scale capital investment — sometimes a billion dollars or more. Construction generates substantial local employment in skilled trades lasting 18–36 months. Property taxes on these massive facilities contribute meaningfully to local school districts and municipal budgets for decades. Without the incentive program, officials argue this investment would simply flow to neighboring states.
The case against: Data centers are among the most highly automated built environments in existence. A 2-million-square-foot campus that required 2,000 workers to construct may permanently employ fewer than 200 people. When you divide the total public subsidy by permanent employees, figures often reach $1–2 million per position. The 2019 program was designed with no environmental conditions attached whatsoever.

Why is AI making this worse — and fast?

For most of the past decade, data center energy consumption grew at a controlled pace — processors were becoming more efficient roughly as fast as data volumes grew. That balance has broken down. The explosion of generative AI requires a categorically different type of computing than traditional internet workloads.

Training a large AI model can consume as much electricity as thousands of households use in a year. And inference — running the model to answer queries — requires massive, continuously operating GPU clusters that consume far more power per unit of work than CPU-based servers. The result: the same companies with the largest Illinois footprints are also the primary developers of the world's most resource-intensive AI systems.

The electricity consequence: Goldman Sachs Research estimated in 2024 that global data center electricity consumption could grow by 160% between 2022 and 2030, with AI workloads as the primary driver. ComEd's load growth projections — the ones that prompted the $4.2 billion emergency grid filing — show a steeper upward curve in 2024–2030 than any previous comparable window in the utility's history.
The water consequence: AI accelerator chips generate substantially more heat per unit of computational work than traditional server CPUs. AI-optimized data centers require more aggressive cooling per megawatt. Either way, the trend is toward more water consumption, not less, as AI workloads displace traditional computing at the facilities this tracker monitors.

Understanding the burden score

This tracker assigns every data center a burden score from 0–100. It is a composite index combining four dimensions of impact into a single comparable number, making it possible to rank facilities and counties against one another in a transparent, defensible, reproducible way.

Energy Draw · 30%

Megawatt capacity from DCEO permits and utility interconnection filings. Weighted equally with water as primary direct environmental stressor.

Water Use · 30%

Estimated using Mytton et al. (2021) formula. Evaporative facilities receive a 1.4× multiplier reflecting substantially higher direct water consumption.

Community Proximity · 25%

Distance to nearest low-income census tract (ACS 2022). EJ flag for facilities within 2 miles of EPA EJScreen 2024-designated communities.

Tax Incentives · 15%

Total certified value of sales tax exemptions under the IL Data Center Investment Program, from DCEO public records.

Methodology

How the burden score is calculated — every weight, every data source, every known limitation — written for journalists, researchers, and anyone who wants to understand not just what the numbers say but how confident we should be in them

The Formula

Burden Score =
  (energy draw × 0.30) +
  (daily water usage × 0.30) +
  (proximity to low-income communities × 0.25) +
  (state tax incentives received × 0.15)

Each variable is normalized to a 0–100 scale using min-max scaling against the full dataset before weighting is applied, so a 500 MW facility and a 50 MW facility can be meaningfully compared within the same index. The weights reflect the relative environmental severity of each factor based on published research in environmental impact assessment and industrial ecology.

Variable Documentation

Energy Draw 30% weight

Megawatt capacity is sourced from Illinois DCEO permit records, utility interconnection queue filings with ComEd and Ameren, operator investor relations disclosures, and trade press in Data Center Knowledge and Datacenter Dynamics. Where a range of capacity is reported (such as "up to 500 MW" for a phased campus), the midpoint is used. Capacity reflects the maximum designed electrical load at full buildout — actual real-time consumption varies by server utilization rate, assumed at 85% for annual calculations, consistent with standard industry benchmarks for mature hyperscale operations.

Daily Water Usage 30% weight

Estimated using the peer-reviewed formula from Mytton, Abubakar, and Bradshaw (2021), Applied Energy: water use intensity of 1.8 liters per kilowatt-hour of IT load, multiplied by facility capacity in MW, multiplied by 24 operating hours per day, multiplied by the 0.85 utilization factor. Evaporative-cooled facilities receive an additional 1.4× multiplier. Uncertainty range: approximately ±25%. When any facility has voluntarily published its own water consumption figures — which is extremely rare — those disclosed numbers are used in preference to the formula estimate. To date, none of the facilities in this dataset have disclosed facility-specific Illinois water use data.

Proximity to Low-Income Communities 25% weight

Measured as straight-line distance from the facility address to the nearest census tract with median household income below 80% of the Area Median Income, using American Community Survey 2022 5-year estimates. The environmental justice overlay adds a binary flag for facilities located within 2 miles of census tracts meeting at least 2 of the 11 cumulative burden indicators in EPA EJScreen 2024. These 11 indicators cover: PM2.5 exposure, ozone, air toxics cancer risk, respiratory hazard index, traffic proximity and volume, proximity to Superfund sites, RMP facilities, hazardous waste treatment facilities, underground storage tank density, wastewater discharge, and drinking water non-compliance.

Tax Incentives Received 15% weight

Total certified incentive value under the Illinois Data Center Investment Program, drawn from DCEO public certification records. These figures represent the estimated total value of sales tax exemptions on qualifying equipment and electricity purchases over the eligible certification period — not a one-time cash payment, but a sustained reduction in the company's tax obligation accumulating over years of operation. Weighted at 15% rather than 25–30% because it represents a fiscal impact on the public treasury rather than a direct physical harm to the environment or to a community's health.

How to Accurately Cite Figures from This Tracker

If you are a journalist, researcher, advocate, or policymaker planning to quote figures from this tracker, the following guidance will help you characterize each data type accurately and defensibly.

Water consumption figures

Always describe as "estimated" or "modeled." Recommended citation: "Estimated at [X] million gallons per day using the Mytton et al. (2021) water use intensity formula, at ±25% uncertainty. Actual consumption has not been publicly disclosed by the operator."

Burden scores

Describe as a "composite burden index" rather than a "score," "grade," or "rating." Recommended citation: "Ranked in the [high/moderate/low] burden tier with a composite index score of [X]/100, based on a weighted formula combining energy draw, estimated water use, proximity to low-income communities, and public incentives received."

Tax incentive figures

Describe as "certified public incentive value" or "maximum certified sales tax exemption." Recommended citation: "Certified for up to $[X] million in state sales tax exemptions under the Illinois Data Center Investment Program, per DCEO public records."

Cost-per-job figures

Always note this reflects permanent jobs only, excluding construction-phase employment. Recommended citation: "The total certified incentive value divided by estimated permanent FTE employees yields a cost per permanent job of approximately $[X]. This excludes temporary construction employment, which typically involves 1,000–2,000 workers over 18–36 months."

The Policy Landscape at a Glance

● Pending State Legislation

IL POWER Act (SB 2342) would require 100% renewable energy for incentivized data centers within 5 years. Governor's FY2026 budget includes 12-month certification moratorium. Neither bill has passed as of this writing. Spring 2025 session is the critical window.

● Active ComEd ICC Docket

ComEd's $4.2B Infrastructure Investment Plan filing argues data centers are the dominant driver of load growth. Core controversy: cost recovery through uniform ratepayer surcharge vs. large-load customer cost allocation. 12–18 month process before final order.

● Enacted County Action

Kane County: 12-month interim moratorium on unincorporated data center permits (Nov. 2024). DeKalb County: hydrogeological study found aquifer stress risk by 2031. Both face jurisdictional limits — incorporated Aurora, Montgomery, and DeKalb city are outside reach.

● Developing Federal Context

EPA EJScreen 2024 designated 14 Illinois tracts near data centers as EJ communities. FERC Order 2023 reforms interconnection queue. Virginia's HB 2217 (mandatory utility impact statements) is a template Illinois advocates are studying closely.
